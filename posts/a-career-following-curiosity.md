[Last month I joined](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/sj-zhang_career-update-ive-joined-alphasense-as-share-7518665036723204096-nctT/) AlphaSense as a Director of Product Design.

Since its founding in 2011, AlphaSense has changed how financial professionals find information and make decisions. Now, AI is changing how the work itself gets done. With 7,000+ enterprise customers, including 90% of the S&P 100, there’s so much opportunity to improve how those firms work. I’m excited to get started.

The last few years at Compound and Coinbase were full of building products, growing teams, and working through customer problems. There’s a lot I could recount here. But starting a new job has me reflecting on something more personal: how following my curiosity shaped the work I took on, what I became good at, and how I could contribute. Looking back, more of those experiences connect to this next chapter than I would have expected.

## Learning Where Value Resides

I joined Compound as its second employee in early 2020. During my time, we grew from zero to $1.5 billion in AUM.

Being part of the founding team, the work went well beyond a traditional design role. I did a ton of product work and watched functions get built from nothing. The team and I designed tools for everything from visualizing startup equity to constructing tax-aware, diversified portfolios.

An early bet of mine was advocating for hiring design engineers and working with them to drive top-of-the-funnel growth in 2021. Along the way, I learned how to attract talent, communicate to the broader market, and develop a voice to drive growth. A merger brought another valuable experience: helping the team navigate the transition across the brand and product.

Most importantly, the job taught me where value actually resides in the industry.

Fintech loves the idea that automation and better software will make financial services radically more efficient. ETFs made passive investing extremely cheap, and robo-advisors made asset allocation easier. In 2020, I shared the expectation of many founders and VCs that automation and lower fees would displace much more of traditional financial advice.

That did not really happen the way I expected.

Wealthy clients remained willing to pay AUM-based fees for human advice. Software could help solve portfolio construction, but that was only a small part of what customers valued.

UHNW clients pay for trust, reassurance, accountability, and a relationship with someone with connections, a personal reputation, or a prestigious name behind them. For someone building substantial wealth through their career or business, an advisory fee actually feels small relative to the financial decisions and fluctuations they are managing. Personal attention, access, and invitations to great seats at an event were all part of the experience. Those things mattered, even if they did not fit neatly into a software efficiency argument.

This was a huge investing lesson. I became extremely sensitive to where a company sits in an industry and which part of the value chain captures the economics. I learned that making an activity more efficient does not necessarily mean capturing its value. In fact, productivity gains often compress margins across an industry, with much of the benefit going to consumers.

## Joining Coinbase and Learning Financial Plumbing

While building tools at Compound to help people be hands-off with their finances, I remained an active trader across markets: equities, options, futures, and crypto. Developing conviction, putting capital behind a bet, and sweating when the market moved against me were all part of the appeal. The combination of macro analysis, political uncertainty, going in and out of leverage at the speed of my finger, and flipping from long to short as news hit the tape kept me both restless and excited.

Working on a broker interface was a natural next step for someone who lives and breathes markets. I joined Coinbase in 2024, a few months after I caught $WIF running and I had become convinced another crypto bull run was taking shape.

My initial focus was Prime Financing, already a sizable business, where I helped grow ARR 10x. After being promoted just a few months in, my scope expanded to trading: spot, futures, perp, and after the Deribit acquisition, options. One highlight was leading the redesign of Prime’s trading interface to support multiple asset classes, derivatives, and trading workflows.

The work was close to my own interests, and my responsibilities naturally kept expanding. What surprised me was how much there was to learn about how money moves within and between financial institutions: lending, trading, custody, clearing, settlement, collateral, and margin accounts at regulated entities. Behind the balance in an app are ledger entries, segregated assets, obligations to external partners, and reconciliation between firms. Who takes each risk, and how much does each participant get paid, and by whom.

What started as my own curiosity became useful to the design team. Sharing that context helped us understand our clients and the financial systems underneath our products. The goal was for everyone to feel more confident in conversations with cross-functional partners and more equipped to question assumptions.

That changed how I looked at my own investments. As someone who frequently traded index ETFs, I used to only think about the return profile and assume every fund would track its index accurately. The trading, rebalancing, reconciliation, and coordination with counterparties required to operate a fund barely crossed my mind. Now, I pay much more attention to who gets paid for each activity and what value they provide in return. It was another lesson in where value resides, this time in Wall Street’s plumbing.

## From Execution Back to Decisions

As I got deeper into execution, AI developed quickly.

Placing and managing orders had changed little for me. From what I’ve seen, agentic trading still has more to prove over algo trading in TradFi and bot trading in crypto. But researching an investment and developing a view felt night-and-day different: I frequently use AI to analyze industries and companies, explore revenue projections, and compare valuation assumptions. Without formal training in building Excel models, these tools made it easier for me to examine ideas. I went as far as building custom dashboards for the companies I followed, organized exactly how I wanted them.

As an investor, I care about execution experience and costs, but the quality of the investment decision has the largest effect on my returns. AI was changing that part of the process, and I wanted to get closer to it.

## Joining AlphaSense

My previous two jobs gave me a foundation in financial services, while investing taught me to think deeply about value creation and capture. At AlphaSense, those experiences come together around a question I already spend a lot of time thinking about: how AI changes the way we understand information, make consequential decisions, and act on them.

**Why vertical AI?**

I believe that, over time, tokens and model capabilities will become increasingly commoditized. Open-source models will continue closing the gap, and the cost of intelligence will move closer to its marginal cost, much as compute and server infrastructure have become a relatively small part of the economics of traditional software.

If that happens, simply wrapping a model around an existing workflow won't be much of a moat. The lasting value in AI software will come from what is harder to replicate: proprietary data, customer trust, established user habits, and the network effects that emerge when people and organizations collaborate through the same platform.

I got to see some of this firsthand through my involvement with OpenEvidence, a medical AI company I started working with in 2024. Rather than building a general-purpose assistant, the team focused relentlessly on physicians: understanding their questions, fitting into their daily work, and earning the trust of the medical community. Today, OpenEvidence is used by more than half of U.S. physicians, becoming a leading AI tool for clinical decision support despite the availability of powerful general-purpose models.

That experience reinforced my belief that the most capable model doesn't necessarily make the most valuable product. I suspect financial services will follow a similar path.

The immediate opportunity for AI software is obvious: help professionals produce the same memos, models, and presentations, only faster and cheaper. The productivity gains are easy to demonstrate, the ROI is easy to explain, and customers understand what they're buying. It's a natural place to start.

But I think the more interesting opportunity is to gradually rethink how work gets done in the first place.

Many of today's workflows and artifacts exist because of the limitations of the tools available when they were created. As AI changes those limitations, we can rethink the process from first principles, starting with what professionals actually need rather than what they're accustomed to producing.

That doesn't mean disregarding how people work today. Behind a financial model, an investment memo, or an approval process are responsibilities, relationships, and competing incentives. What looks like an efficiency problem may actually be a human problem.

That's what attracts me to vertical AI and AlphaSense: the opportunity to understand those needs deeply enough to change how work gets done, gradually earning the trust and habits that make a product indispensable. I want to help build toward a future where AlphaSense doesn't just help professionals find information or produce work faster, but becomes part of how firms go from research to decisions to actually getting things done.

Looking back, the connections are clearer than they were at the time. None of this was perfectly planned. Following my curiosity kept leading me toward work I enjoyed, could get good at, and could contribute more through. That's something I want to keep following into this next chapter.
